# Rough timeline
1. Out of Africa. The starting point. Humans spread through Eurasia, learning fire, stone tools, and hunting. The first ledger entries come from local game, such as cave bears and woolly rhinos in Europe.

2. Across the Sea. Humans reach Australia by boat. Within a few thousand years of arrival, the continent loses most of its giant animals: Diprotodon (a rhino-sized marsupial), Genyornis (a giant flightless bird), Thylacoleo (the marsupial lion), and Megalania (a giant monitor lizard). Fire use changes the landscape as well, reshaping the forests that remain.

2. A First Footprints. Boats carry people across narrow sea gaps from Southeast Asia to the continent of Sahul, which included Australia and New Guinea as a single landmass during lower sea levels. The first upgrade is simply “arrival,” which unlocks coastal settlement. No ledger entries yet, which lets the player feel the new world before the cost appears.

2. B Fire Country. People burn the land deliberately to flush game, clear paths, and encourage new growth. Burning opens grasslands and thins forests. Upgrade trade-off: better hunting yields and wider range, paid for with shifting vegetation and fewer fire-sensitive plants. This is a good early example of a “quiet” cost.

2. C The Megafauna Decline. Giant animals start disappearing. The ledger entries, with what the game should model as each one’s pressure:

Diprotodon, a rhinoceros-sized marsupial that could weigh around 2 to 3 tonnes. Slow breeding makes it vulnerable to hunting.
Genyornis, a flightless bird roughly two metres tall. Its eggs and nesting grounds were exposed to hunting and disturbance.
Megalania, a giant monitor lizard. Its predatory role may have made it sensitive to losing prey.
Thylacoleo, the marsupial lion, which relied on large prey that was declining.
Procoptodon, a giant short-faced kangaroo that browsed on open plant life.

2. D Refuges. Some animals and plants survive in wetter pockets, rainforest edges, and rugged terrain. This stage is a mechanic for regional refuges: the player can keep species alive in them, and the ledger shows which species were never fully lost. The lesson is that damage is patchy, and survival depends on habitat.

3. Into the Americas. Hunters spread across North and South America. Mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths, Smilodon (saber-toothed cats), and American horses decline. Each region loses different species, which adds variety to the ledger.

3. The Mammoth Steppe (new)
Across northern Eurasia lay a cold, dry grassland supporting woolly mammoths, woolly rhinos, cave lions, and steppe bison. Humans adapt with tailored clothing, sewn shelters, and better fire management. Upgrades here unlock Ice Age regions, and the ledger starts recording local losses such as the cave hyena and cave bear in Europe, which share the same prey base.

4. Island Hopping. Humans reach islands with species that never learned to fear them. Madagascar loses its giant lemurs and elephant birds, and the Pacific islands lose flightless birds such as the moa in New Zealand and the dodo on Mauritius. Island extinctions are some of the fastest in the game, which makes them a strong lesson.

4. Beringia (new)
Lower sea levels exposed a land bridge between Siberia and Alaska. Humans cross it and wait in the region for a time, possibly for thousands of years, before moving south. This stage can have a unique mechanic: the player has to decide whether to push through quickly, which unlocks the Americas sooner but leaves the region’s species under more pressure, or to hold back. Holding back is slower but preserves more of the local ecosystem.

5. The Last Mammoths. A mechanic that shows survival matters: small isolated populations hang on for a while. Woolly mammoths on Wrangel Island persisted long after the mainland populations were gone. The player can see the last remnant before it disappears, which makes the ledger feel personal.

6. First Farms. Agriculture replaces hunting in many regions. Forests and soil take the cost: clearing land, soil loss, and salinization in river valleys. Animals that depended on wild grasslands or forests start to decline.

7. Trade and Empire. Ships and trade routes spread humans and their animals and plants worldwide. Invasive species such as rats, goats, and cats reach islands and drive native birds and reptiles to extinction. Whaling and overfishing reduce large sea animals, and Steller’s sea cow is hunted to extinction within decades of its discovery.

8. The Industrial Surge. Coal, steam, and mechanized farming multiply output. Forest cover drops, rivers are dammed, and large animals such as the passenger pigeon and Carolina parakeet are driven to extinction by hunting and habitat loss. Pollution becomes a visible ledger category.

9. The Global Age. Antibiotics, plastics, and aviation drive a population boom. Biodiversity loss spreads to nearly every region, and the ledger fills faster than the player can track it. Conservation tech appears here, but it costs growth and only partly offsets the losses.

10. The Ledger. The endgame. The player sees the full record and the regional health maps, and chooses how to close out the run. A prestige reset can show how different choices would have changed the outcome.
